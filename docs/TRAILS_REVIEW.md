## Austin — The 250 Trail: Austin — East Side Standards · factory (shipped with batch-2)

> **Hook:** Walk the East 11th and 12th spine where Black and Mexican-American Austin built the warmth the whole city later called 'weird' — from a 1945 Chitlin' Circuit juke joint to the brisket and masa masters who planted on the same blocks.

**Neighborhoods:** Central East Austin (East 11th/12th) · East Cesar Chavez · Rosewood / Holy Cross · **Narrative:** 209 words · **Stops:** 7

Start where the music started. The Historic Victory Grill opened on V-J Day, 1945, when Johnny Holmes lit up East 11th Street for Black soldiers coming home to a segregated city — and it became one of Austin's essential stops on the Chitlin' Circuit, the juke-joint network that carried B.B. King, Bobby 'Blue' Bland and Gatemouth Brown across the South. It landed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1998, and it's still standing. That East 11th and 12th corridor is the spine of this walk: Black and Mexican-American Austin built here, fed each other here, and kept the porch light on through decades of change. A few blocks over, Joe's Bakery has flipped migas since the Avila family named it in 1962 — a line running back to a 1935 East 9th grocery, and forward to a 2023 James Beard America's Classic. This is the soil the come-as-you-are Austin ethos actually grew from. When the Armadillo World Headquarters opened in a converted 1940s armory in 1970, it borrowed that no-pretension warmth and made it legend before closing in 1980. The Armadillo's gone; the inheritance isn't. You'll taste it in Aaron Franklin's brisket line — East 11th since 2011 — and in Edgar Rico's masa a mile east.

## Chicago — The 250 Trail: Chicago — The Neighborhood Mosaic · **NEW (sign-off required)**

> **Hook:** One city, 77 official pieces, each built by somebody who just got here. Eat the mosaic — Polish sausage to carnitas, dim sum to glögg — on the streets where the blues plugged in.

**Neighborhoods:** Maxwell Street / Near West Side · Pilsen · Chinatown · Bronzeville · Andersonville · **Narrative:** 233 words · **Stops:** 9

Chicago keeps its books in neighborhoods — 77 official community areas, mapped in the 1920s and barely changed since, each one a ledger of who arrived and what they built. This trail reads a few pages. Start at Maxwell Street, where a Macedonian immigrant named Jimmy Stefanovic bought his aunt's hot dog stand in 1939 and — by most tellings — invented the Maxwell Street Polish in 1943; the same market where bluesmen up from the Delta plugged into borrowed extension cords so the guitar could carry over the haggling. That corner is the whole city in miniature: everybody's food, everybody's music, volume up. From there, Pilsen — named by Czech immigrants for Plzeň, remade by Mexican families in the 1960s, home to a free-admission museum of Mexican art founded in 1982 by educators who pooled $900. Then Chinatown, planted at Cermak and Wentworth in 1912, where the On Leong Merchants Association raised its own building and made a neighborhood. Then Bronzeville, the Black Metropolis built by the Great Migration — more than 100,000 Black Chicagoans by 1920, and the sound they carried north still plays live every night. Finish where a Swedish tavern has poured since 1934 up in Andersonville — after a Ukrainian Village tasting counter that carried Filipino cooking to a Michelin star. No single stop is the point here. The mosaic is. Come hungry; it's a long, wide city.

## Denver — The 250 Trail: Denver · factory (shipped with batch-2)

> **Hook:** Welton Street was the Harlem of the West. Follow the music from Five Points' jazz corridor into RiNo's mural economy — one century, one improvised neighborhood.

**Neighborhoods:** Five Points · RiNo (River North Art District) · Park Hill · **Narrative:** 208 words · **Stops:** 8

Basecamp rule for this one: hydrate before you sip. You're at 5,280 feet and the Welton Street sun is deceptively strong, so start with tea, not a stout. Five Points earned its 'Harlem of the West' nickname for good reason — by 1920 more than ninety percent of Denver's Black residents lived here, and Welton Street pulsed with better than fifty jazz clubs and bars. Barred from whites-only downtown hotels, touring giants like Duke Ellington, Ella Fitzgerald and Nat King Cole slept and played at the Rossonian at 2642 Welton, while the Casino Cabaret — opened by Denver's 'Godfather of Jazz,' George Morrison, and today reborn as Cervantes' Masterpiece Ballroom — kept the marquee lit for decades. This trail starts on that living corridor, where Black-owned businesses still steep community, then bends northwest into RiNo, where the old warehouse district trades saxophones for spray paint. Larimer Street is the new stage: each fall the Denver Walls festival repaints the block, and the breweries wear those murals like liner notes. Walk it in the morning's cool, chase shade between stops, and let the afternoon build toward dinner. Same neighborhood energy, a century apart — improvised, hand-built, unmistakably Denver. Bring layers; the mountain air turns on you fast after sundown.

## Houston — The 250 Trail: Houston — The City That Eats in Every Language · **NEW (sign-off required)**

> **Hook:** The most diverse big city in America wrote its menu in every language it speaks. Drive the corridors where immigration did the cooking — from the Navigation Boulevard taqueria that taught America to crave tacos al carbon to Viet-Cajun crawfish on Bellaire.

**Neighborhoods:** East End / Navigation Boulevard · EaDo · Montrose · Mahatma Gandhi District · Asiatown / Bellaire Boulevard · **Narrative:** 202 words · **Stops:** 9

Start with the number the census keeps confirming: greater Houston is the most racially and ethnically diverse of America's largest metro areas, with more than 145 languages spoken across the region. Then go taste why. In 1973, on Navigation Boulevard in the East End, a widow named Ninfa Laurenzo started grilling tacos al carbon in front of her tortilla factory and taught the whole country to say fajita. Two years later Saigon fell, and Houston became one of the nation's great Vietnamese resettlement cities — home today to the largest Vietnamese population outside California — whose cooks would eventually toss Gulf crawfish in garlic butter and lemongrass and hand Texas a brand-new craving. On Hillcroft, the city put it in writing in 2010, raising street signs that read Mahatma Gandhi District over blocks of biryani, chaat, and gold jewelry. And down Bellaire Boulevard, Asiatown runs for miles of strip-mall brilliance, from Sichuan chili oil to Malaysian roti. This trail is a drive, not a stroll — Houston is honest about that — so bring a car, a big appetite, and people willing to split everything. Order what the next table is having. That's how this menu got written in the first place.

## Kansas City — The 250 Trail: Kansas City · factory (shipped with batch-2)

> **Hook:** Follow the smoke from the jazz clubs of 18th & Vine to the pits that same neighborhood built — one long, low-and-slow lineage of Black Kansas City genius.

**Neighborhoods:** 18th & Vine · East Side · Crossroads Arts District · **Narrative:** 214 words · **Stops:** 8

Some things you can't rush. A brisket takes all night, and so did this neighborhood's music — the two came up together, in the same blocks, off the same restless brilliance. In the 1920s and '30s, 18th & Vine was the beating heart of Black Kansas City: a jazz town where Count Basie and Charlie Parker cut their teeth, and where, by some counts, close to a hundred barbecue joints crowded the same streets. Henry Perry, a Tennessee man who came to town in 1907, sold smoked meat from a stand near 18th & Vine and later worked a trolley barn at 19th and Highland — earning the title Father of Kansas City barbecue. His apprentices carried the fire forward: Arthur and Charlie Bryant, and Arthur Pinkard, who first cooked for Gates. This trail runs that lineage low and slow. Start where the music lives — the American Jazz Museum, the Negro Leagues Baseball Museum next door, the after-hours jam at the Mutual Musicians Foundation, a union hall since 1917 and the oldest continuously running African-American club in the country. Then follow your nose to the pits. I won't tell you which burnt end wins; that's a debate best settled with a full plate and an open mind. Just come hungry, and come patient.

**Neighborhoods:** El Pueblo / Olvera Street · Boyle Heights · Arts District · Highland Park · **Narrative:** 228 words · **Stops:** 7

Los Angeles began on September 4, 1781, when forty-four settlers — most of them of Indigenous and African descent, come north from Sonora and Sinaloa — founded a pueblo near the river. Nearly two and a half centuries later, the blocks just east of that spot still hold the city's memory. Start at Olvera Street, the marketplace that has sold taquitos and remembrance since Easter Sunday 1930. Then cross the river into Boyle Heights, where mariachis in charro suits have gathered near the old Boyle Hotel since the 1930s, hired song by song, and where a neighborhood once counted among the most multiethnic in America — Mexican, Jewish, Japanese, Armenian, Russian — taught this city how to share a table. Eat slow-braised guisados on handmade tortillas from a father-and-son shop on Cesar Chavez Avenue. Find the truck a Jalisco-born cook parked on Olympic in 2002 and order the shrimp tacos dorados twice. Watch what happens when a kid from Pico Rivera turns a $300 street cart into an Arts District institution. Then ride Figueroa — the old Route 66 — up into Highland Park and finish inside a bowling alley that opened in 1927. Bring small bills for the trucks, share everything, and if a mariachi offers a song, say yes and tip well. This is the east side that fed Los Angeles first — and never stopped.

## Miami — The 250 Trail: Miami — Two Exile Capitals · **NEW (sign-off required)**

> **Hook:** Two capitals-in-exile, a few miles apart — cafecito windows and domino tables on Calle Ocho, griot and kompa up Northeast Second. Walk the axis where two islands rebuilt their homelands and remade an American city.

**Neighborhoods:** Little Havana / Calle Ocho · Little Haiti / NE 2nd Avenue · Little River · **Narrative:** 220 words · **Stops:** 9

Miami runs on two engines built by two islands. The first fires on Calle Ocho, where Cubans who left after 1959 rebuilt their capital from memory: Felipe Valls Sr. opened Versailles in 1971 and helped turn the ventanita — the walk-up cafecito window — into the city's town square, while the domino tables at Máximo Gómez Park have clacked on the corner of Eighth and Fifteenth since the early seventies. Down the block, Ball & Chain opened as a saloon in 1935; Billie Holiday and Count Basie played that room long before it became the neighborhood's salsa floor. The second engine hums a few miles northeast, in what was Lemon City — a settlement older than Miami itself. Haitian families arriving in the 1970s and '80s made it their main street; when community elder Viter Juste called it 'Little Port-au-Prince' in the Miami Herald, editors shortened the name to Little Haiti, and in 2016 the city made it official. Wilkinson Sejour has fried griot there since 1992, kompa still pours out of storefronts along Northeast Second Avenue, and lately a Michelin star hides in a neighborhood strip mall. This trail runs the axis between the two — cafecito to griot, son to kompa — and lands on one very American lesson: bring everything you love with you, then share it.

## Nashville — The 250 Trail: Nashville · factory (shipped with batch-2)

> **Hook:** Before Music Row had a sound, Jefferson Street had the song. Follow the R&B corridor and the writers' rooms as one long verse.

**Neighborhoods:** Jefferson Street / North Nashville · Downtown / SoBro · Music Row · Green Hills · The Gulch · **Narrative:** 197 words · **Stops:** 8

Start where the labels won't tell you the story began: Jefferson Street. Before Music Row, this corridor by Fisk, Meharry, and TSU held clubs — the Del Morocco, Club Baron — where a young Jimi Hendrix played guitar in a house band before Interstate 40 cut the neighborhood's heart out in the late 1960s. We follow what survived. At the National Museum of African American Music, opened downtown in 2021, you trace fifty-plus genres — spirituals to hip-hop — back to their makers. Up the hill, Fisk's Jubilee Singers carried Black spirituals around the world beginning in 1871, their proceeds funding the university; the same campus is where Aaron Douglas built the art program and painted his murals. Then eat where the movement ate: Swett's has ladled soul food since 1954. Chase the burn at Prince's, the family credited with inventing hot chicken. From there, the writers' rooms — Historic RCA Studio B, where Chet Atkins and Elvis cut the Nashville Sound; the Ryman, the Mother Church, where a hush still falls mid-song; the Bluebird, where the song, not the singer, runs the room; and the Station Inn, keeping bluegrass's all-night vigil. Same city, one unbroken line.

## New Orleans — The 250 Trail: New Orleans · **NEW (sign-off required)**

> **Hook:** Three hundred years of French, Spanish, African, Caribbean, and Acadian hands in the same pot. Walk from Congo Square's drum circles to the po-boy counters and the families still stirring the roux.

**Neighborhoods:** Tremé · French Quarter · Mid-City · Warehouse District · **Narrative:** 217 words · **Stops:** 9

Come hungry, pace yourself, and know that the correct answer to 'Creole or Cajun?' is yes.

## Smoky Mountains — The 250 Trail: Smoky Mountains — Handmade in the Hollers · **NEW (sign-off required)**

Fiddle-and-banjo string bands and the hymns raised in congregations as old as Cades Cove's Primitive Baptist, organized in 1827, ran down the generations until a girl from Locust Ridge named Dolly Parton carried the mountains to the world's stage — then came home and put her name on the park in 1986. Drive it, don't rush it — Glades shops keep their own hours — and let the best souvenir be something with the maker's name on it.
